Concept 1 · The foundation

Surplus Value

Imagine you work in a bakery. In one hour, you turn £10 worth of flour, yeast, and electricity into £30 worth of bread.

The £20 of extra value — that came from your labour. You made something worth more than the ingredients.

Now, your boss pays you £15 an hour. You created £20 of new value but only got £15 back. Where did the other £5 go?

That £5 is surplus value. It's the gap between what your labour produced and what you were paid for it.

The boss keeps it. That's where profit comes from — not from the ingredients (those were already priced in), not from the oven (that's a tool), but from the difference between what you made and what you were paid.

Here's the uncomfortable part: this isn't about a bad boss. A good boss, a fair boss, a boss who gives you a Christmas bonus — the structure is the same. If you got back everything your labour produced, there'd be nothing left for the business. Profit requires that gap.

Marx's question was: is that fair? You made the bread. The value came from your hands and your time. Why does someone who owns the oven and the flour get to keep part of what you created?

The Answer

Because they own the means of production and you don't. You sell your time because you have to — you can't eat without a wage. They buy your time because it's worth more than they pay for it. The arrangement benefits one side more than the other, and it's not a coincidence which side is which.

In one sentence: Surplus value is the difference between what workers produce and what they're paid, and it's the source of capitalist profit.

Concept 2 · How change works

Dialectical Materialism

Click to expand ↓

You know how people argue about whether it's "human nature" that causes poverty, or "bad policies," or "greedy corporations"?

Marx said: stop looking for one single cause. Reality doesn't work like that.

Everything that exists — the economy, the government, the culture, the technology, the way people think — is connected to everything else, and it's all in constant motion. Nothing stays still. Nothing is permanent. Everything is becoming something else.

That's the "dialectical" part: reality is a process, not a snapshot. Things change because they contain contradictions — opposing forces pulling in different directions. Capitalism creates enormous wealth AND enormous poverty. Those aren't accidents; they're connected, and the tension between them drives the system forward.

The Materialism Part

Before you think about ideas, culture, religion, or politics, look at how people are actually surviving. How are they getting food, shelter, warmth? Who owns the tools and land? Who works? Who takes the surplus?

The material conditions — the real, physical setup of how people produce what they need — come first. The ideas, laws, religions, and political systems grow on top of that. Change the foundation and the building has to change too.

But it's not a one-way street. Material conditions shape ideas, but ideas can feed back and change the material conditions. A political ideology can unite workers, who then change the material setup by seizing the means of production. It's a loop, not a line.

In one sentence: Reality is made of interconnected, constantly-changing processes driven by internal contradictions, and the most fundamental driver is how people produce what they need to survive.
